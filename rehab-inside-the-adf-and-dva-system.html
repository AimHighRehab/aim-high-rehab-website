Inside ADF and DVA Rehab: What Civilian Providers Rarely See

Working defence force files taught me things no civilian caseload ever could.

Most rehabilitation consultants never work an ADF file. I'd genuinely recommend every provider in this space try to, even briefly — it reframes how you think about recovery and motivation.

A completely different incentive structure

In civilian workers compensation, it's rare to meet someone pushing to get back to work earlier than medically advised. In defence, it's the opposite. Members on crutches would ask when they could get back on deployment — because deployment pay is meaningfully higher, and tax-free. For some, it's one of a limited number of deployments left in a career, and that has real financial weight: paying off a house, funding a child's education.

But it's not purely financial. There's a strong pull toward service itself — patriotism, identity, a sense of purpose tied to the uniform. Untangling which motivation is driving a member's urgency to return is part of the job, and it's rarely just one or the other.

The gap that worries me most

The hardest part of this work isn't the injuries — it's the handover. When a member is medically discharged, responsibility is meant to pass to the Department of Veterans' Affairs. In practice, that handover can be little more than a message passed along, and if it gets lost, a person who served for most of their adult life can fall through the cracks entirely — sometimes ending up isolated, or worse.

There wasn't really a durability check — no one calling six months later just to see how they were going.

That gap is a structural one, not a bad-actor one. Everyone involved is doing their part of the process; it's the seams between organisations where people get lost.

Why this experience changes how I work everywhere else

Seeing land-based mental health struggles up close — the frustration of an active-duty member sidelined by injury, desperate to rejoin their unit — gave me a much sharper sense of how much identity and purpose factor into recovery, in every setting, not just defence. It's part of why "return to life before return to work" isn't just a phrase for me — it's something I watched play out in real time, with real consequences, in a system most rehab providers never get to see from the inside.
