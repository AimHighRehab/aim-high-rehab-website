The Hidden Cost of Being Believed

Sometimes the biggest barrier to recovery isn't physical. It's whether the people around you take the injury seriously.

Invisible injuries carry a particular kind of weight. Unlike a cast or a set of crutches, a lot of the conditions I treat — back injuries, nerve pain, complex or chronic presentations — leave no visible evidence for coworkers to see. And when there's nothing to see, people sometimes assume there's nothing there.

A small moment that changed how I think about this

I've run treatment sessions directly at a client's workplace before — turning up to do an exercise physiology session on-site. What struck me wasn't the treatment itself, but the reaction from coworkers watching a practitioner arrive. The presence alone changed something: it signalled, without anyone having to say it, that the injury was real and being taken seriously. Teasing or quiet scepticism tends to fade once colleagues see that the process is being handled properly.

Bringing someone into the process

I encourage injured workers to bring a support person — a partner, a friend, a family member — into appointments and case conferences wherever appropriate. It's not just emotional support. When people close to the situation understand exactly where someone is in their recovery and what the plan actually is, it removes a lot of the unspoken suspicion that builds when recovery isn't visible or well understood from the outside.

Why this matters as much as the clinical side

Recovery isn't purely physical. Feeling disbelieved by coworkers, family, or even case managers adds a psychological weight on top of an already difficult recovery — and that weight can slow progress just as much as the injury itself. Part of a rehab consultant's job is making sure every stakeholder — employer, insurer, GP, family — has a clear, consistent picture of where someone actually is, so no one has to carry the injury and the burden of proving it's real.
